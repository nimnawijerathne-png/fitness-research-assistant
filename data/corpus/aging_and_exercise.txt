Exercise Considerations for Aging Adults

Sarcopenia — the age-related loss of muscle mass and strength — typically begins around age 30 and accelerates after age 60 if not counteracted by regular resistance training, making strength training increasingly important, not less, as people age.

Resistance training in older adults has been consistently shown to improve muscle strength, bone density, balance, and functional independence, and is associated with reduced fall risk — a leading cause of injury and loss of independence in older populations.

Bone density also naturally declines with age, particularly in post-menopausal women due to reduced estrogen; weight-bearing and resistance exercise are among the few non-pharmacological interventions shown to help slow this decline.

Recovery between training sessions may need to be somewhat longer for older adults due to naturally reduced recovery capacity, though this varies significantly based on training history and overall health rather than age alone.

Balance and mobility-focused exercises (e.g. single-leg stances, controlled multi-directional movement) are commonly recommended alongside strength training for older adults specifically to address fall-risk factors that general strength training alone may not fully target.
