Alcohol's Effects on Fitness and Recovery

Alcohol consumption, particularly in the hours after resistance training, has been shown in research to impair muscle protein synthesis, potentially blunting some of the muscle repair and growth response to a workout, even when adequate protein is consumed.

Alcohol disrupts sleep architecture, particularly reducing REM sleep, even when it may help someone fall asleep faster — this is relevant given sleep's central role in physical recovery, as covered in general sleep and recovery guidance.

Alcohol is calorically dense (approximately 7 calories per gram, second only to fat) and provides no meaningful nutritional value, meaning regular consumption can meaningfully impact caloric balance and interfere with weight management goals.

Acute alcohol intake has been associated with impaired coordination, reaction time, and thermoregulation, which is part of why exercising while intoxicated increases injury risk, and why the day following heavier drinking often sees impaired workout performance even without a formal hangover.

Moderate alcohol intake, as generally defined by public health guidelines (though these vary by country), is less consistently linked to major negative fitness outcomes compared to heavier or more frequent consumption, but no level of alcohol intake actively improves athletic performance or recovery.
