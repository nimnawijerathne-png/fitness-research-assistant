Exercise and Mental Health

Regular physical activity is consistently associated with reduced symptoms of anxiety and depression across a large body of research, with effect sizes in some studies comparable to certain first-line treatments for mild-to-moderate depression, though exercise is generally recommended as a complement to, not a replacement for, professional mental health treatment when needed.

Both aerobic exercise and resistance training have shown mental health benefits, suggesting the effect is not limited to one exercise modality; consistency of practice appears more important than the specific type of exercise chosen.

Proposed mechanisms behind exercise's mental health benefits include increased production of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF, which supports neuron growth and connectivity), regulation of stress hormones like cortisol, improved sleep quality, and psychosocial factors such as social interaction and a sense of accomplishment.

Even single bouts of moderate exercise have been shown to produce short-term reductions in anxiety and improvements in mood, distinct from the longer-term benefits of a consistent exercise habit.

Over-exercising, particularly when combined with inadequate recovery, can have the opposite effect — contributing to increased fatigue, irritability, and in some cases symptoms overlapping with burnout, underscoring that moderate, sustainable activity levels are more strongly linked to mental health benefits than extreme training volumes.
