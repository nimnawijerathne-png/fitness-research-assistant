Mobility and Stretching

Mobility refers to the ability to move a joint actively through its full range of motion with control, which differs from flexibility (the passive ability of a muscle to lengthen). Good mobility depends on both flexibility and adequate strength at end ranges of motion.

Static stretching involves holding a muscle in a lengthened position, typically for 20-60 seconds, and is generally most beneficial when performed after exercise or during dedicated mobility sessions, where it can contribute to long-term flexibility improvements.

Dynamic stretching involves moving a joint through its range of motion in a controlled, repetitive manner (e.g. leg swings, arm circles) and is generally preferred before exercise, as it raises tissue temperature and prepares muscles for activity without the temporary strength reduction associated with pre-exercise static stretching.

Limited mobility in certain joints (commonly the hips, ankles, and thoracic spine) is often associated with compensatory movement patterns elsewhere in the body during exercises like squats, which can increase injury risk over time if not addressed.

Foam rolling and other self-myofascial release techniques are commonly used to temporarily reduce muscle tightness and improve range of motion, though research on their long-term structural effects on tissue is limited; their main established benefit appears to be short-term improvements in mobility and perceived muscle soreness.
